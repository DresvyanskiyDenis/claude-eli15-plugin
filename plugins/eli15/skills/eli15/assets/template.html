Queues · why waiting is not linear

A café line explodes when the barista is 90% busy

The wait does not grow in step with how busy the barista is. It grows with busy ÷ spare, so the last few points of load cost the most: 8 minutes at 80% busy, 18 at 90%.

Model: one barista, customers and drink times both random (the M/M/1 queue), 2-minute average drink. All numbers are long-run averages.

The surprise

Load and wait do not grow in step

Guess firstThe barista takes 2 minutes per drink and is busy 90% of the time. How long does the average customer wait in line?

Show the answer

About 18 minutes, nine drinks' worth. Most people guess 2 to 4, because they picture the line growing in step with the load. The next figure shows how far apart the two really are.

Myth

If the barista is 10% busier, customers wait about 10% longer.

What happens

The wait goes from 8 to 18 minutes. The reason is the next section: what clears a line is the barista's spare time, and spare time halves from 20% to 10%.

The mechanism

Spare time is the only thing that clears a backlog

Customers do not arrive evenly: sometimes five walk in together. The barista can work off that extra line only in the minutes the normal flow of orders leaves free. At 50% busy, half of every minute is free. At 90% busy, a tenth is.

Key idea

The wait formula is this picture in one line: average wait = drink time × busy ÷ spare. At 90%: 2 min × 0.9 ÷ 0.1 = 18 min. As spare time shrinks toward zero, the wait has no ceiling.

Where the formula comes from

For random arrivals and drink times (the M/M/1 queue), the average number of people waiting is ρ21−ρ, where ρ is the busy share. Little's law says people waiting = arrivals per minute × wait, and arrivals per minute are ρ/S for drink time S. Divide, and the wait is

Wq=S·ρ1−ρ

The fix

One shared line halves the wait without hiring anyone

Take two baristas, each 90% busy. With two separate lines, one barista can stand idle while people wait in the other line. With one shared line, nobody waits while a barista is free.

Watch out

Real customers switch to the shorter line, which closes part of this gap. A shared line still wins, because nobody gets stuck behind one slow order while the other barista is free.

The limits

Real lines are usually worse than this model

Every number above assumes drink times vary at random around 2 minutes. If they vary more, the whole curve lifts. A flat white takes one minute and a seasonal special takes six.

  • People leave when the line looks long. That caps the line but loses the sale, so the café sees a shorter line than the model and less revenue.
  • Rush hour is not steady. The formula gives the long-run average. A 20-minute rush can run well above it and take an hour to drain.
  • Arrivals are not always random. A bus stop outside the door sends customers in clumps, and clumps lengthen the wait the same way erratic drinks do.
The formula with variability

Kingman's approximation multiplies the basic wait by the average squared spread of arrival gaps ca and drink times cs (1 means random, 0 means perfectly regular):

Wq≈S·ρ1−ρ·ca2+cs22

What to remember

Wait is drink time × busy ÷ spare

  1. From 80% to 90% busy, the wait more than doubles: 8 to 18 minutes.
  2. Only spare time drains a backlog, and at 90% busy there is a tenth of it.
  3. One shared line cuts the wait from 18 to 8.5 minutes with the same staff.
  4. Uneven drink times or clumped arrivals push every number higher.

Ask about your own line: how busy is your busiest hour, not your average one? The average hides the hour where the curve goes vertical.
